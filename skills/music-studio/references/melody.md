# Melody

The melody is what people remember. Write it first, alone, and test it before orchestrating anything.

## What makes a hook

- **A motif of 2 to 6 notes with an identity.** Identity comes from rhythm first (a dotted figure, a syncopation, a long-short pattern), then from one characteristic interval (a rising fourth, fifth or sixth, a falling minor third).
- **A narrow range.** A singable melody spans an octave, a tenth at most. The hook itself often spans a fifth or sixth.
- **Mostly steps, a few leaps.** Steps make it singable; one leap makes it memorable. After a leap, move by step in the opposite direction (gap-fill).
- **Repetition.** The motif comes back at least twice in the phrase, often three times. Listeners need to recognise before they can love.
- **A question and an answer.** The first phrase ends open (on the 2nd or 5th degree, over V or IV); the answer ends closed (on the tonic or the 3rd, over I). Same opening, different ending is the strongest pattern.
- **A high point placed late.** The highest note comes once, around two thirds into the phrase or the section, on a strong beat and a long value. A melody that peaks early has nowhere to go.
- **Rhythmic contrast within the phrase.** Short notes that lead to long ones. Ending a phrase on a long note gives the listener time to breathe and the orchestra time to answer.

## Phrase shapes

| Shape | Bars | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Period: antecedent + consequent | 4 + 4 | the default: question, answer |
| Sentence: idea, idea repeated, continuation | 2 + 2 + 4 | drives forward; good for themes that build |
| AABA | 8 × 4 | song form, waltzes, ballads |
| Call and response | 1 + 1 or 2 + 2 | between two sections of the orchestra (horns call, strings answer) |

## Developing a motif

A whole cue can grow from one motif. Use these, in this order of subtlety:

1. **Repeat** it in a new register or orchestration.
2. **Sequence** it: the same shape starting a step or a third higher, two or three times, to build tension.
3. **Fragment** it: keep the first two or three notes, repeat them faster as the energy rises.
4. **Augment** it (longer values) for majesty at the climax; **diminish** it (shorter values) for urgency.
5. **Invert** the contour (rising becomes falling) for a darker or answering version.
6. **Re-harmonise** it: the same notes over a new chord (a major melody over a minor chord, the melody note becoming a 9th or a suspension) changes its meaning completely.
7. **Change mode**: the hero's theme in minor for the defeat, back in major for the victory.

## Checks before going on

- Hum it in your head at tempo: does it have a rhythm you would tap?
- Range within an octave or a tenth? The high point once, late, long?
- The motif heard at least twice? The phrase ends on a long note?
- Long notes on chord tones or on tensions that resolve by step (a suspension 4-3, an appoggiatura 9-8)?
- Does it fit the instrument that will play it (`orchestration.md` ranges), and the singers' syllables if the choir sings it?
- Does it sound like a famous tune? If so, change two of rhythm, contour, starting degree or leap (see `method.md`).

## Pitfalls

- **Stepwise wandering** with no motif: it sounds like library music.
- **Arpeggio melodies** that only spell the chord: they sound like an accompaniment.
- **Every note the same length**: no rhythm, no identity.
- **Too many ideas**: one motif, developed, beats four motifs stated.
- **A sung melody that moves fast on one vowel**: it sounds like an organ; give the choir held notes and syllables instead.
- **A melody buried in its own register**: if the accompaniment plays in the melody's octave, move the accompaniment, not the melody.
